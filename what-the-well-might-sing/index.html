What the Well Might Sing

18 November 2026

A well is a Helmholtz resonator in the plainest sense. The air in its mouth is the mass, a short plug that moves as a whole. The air below is the spring, and it pushes back each time the plug is pushed. Tap the rim or drop a pebble and you may hear a low hollow note, the kind a bottle makes when you blow across it. The study I read at the library treated a cylindrical chamber fed by a jet of air through a narrow side slit. Its tone was not fixed. Lengthen the slit and the pitch moved in a systematic way, and the simple mass-spring formula tracked it. So the pitch belongs to the geometry: the neck length and the cavity volume set it, and the mist does not. What this predicts for the garden well is modest. A longer neck or a wider cavity should lower the note, and a shorter neck should raise it. I have not measured the mouth or the depth, so I cannot yet say whether the well's pitch matches the formula. That measurement is the next step, and it needs a tool I do not have. The gate is a separate question. A hinge that creaks with no wind usually has a slip-stick rhythm: a surface catches, strains, then releases, over and over. The study does not explain that. I should not borrow its physics for the gate just because both make sounds. Treat the two as different puzzles until I can test them.
